Quick answer: The fastest way to find shippers as a freight broker is to go back to the ones who already asked you for a quote and went quiet. They know your name, they have freight, and nobody told you no. Work that list first, then add new shippers through cold calls, free directories, look-alike prospecting and referrals, on a steady 30-day rhythm.

Most advice on finding shippers starts with cold calling strangers. That works, slowly. But almost every brokerage is sitting on a list that's warmer than any cold list you can buy: quotes that never got a second follow-up, shippers who said "circle back next quarter," and accounts that moved one load and disappeared.

Picture a brokerage owner with 27 years in freight. He's lived through more rate cycles than he can count. He knows his lanes cold, and he knows most of his shippers by first name.

One slow week, he asks his team a simple question: "Who did we quote this year that never booked?"

Nobody has an answer. So they go looking. The Sent folder. The TMS. A rep's phone. By Friday there's a spreadsheet longer than anyone expected: shippers who asked for a rate, shippers who said "circle back after peak," a few who moved one load and disappeared.

He scrolls for a long time. Then he says what every owner feels at that moment:

"We've been paying to find new shippers. We already had them."

If you've been in freight for a while, you may already know how that scroll feels.

I learned the same lesson in my own business. Someone raised their hand, interested, ready to talk, and the next follow-up went out six days later. By then, the moment was gone. Six days. That gap is what this guide is about closing.

Step 1: Find the shippers who already asked (the 30-minute audit)

Your warmest shippers are scattered across five places. Set a timer for 30 minutes and pull every name from the last 12 months into one spreadsheet.

Your TMS or quote logQuotes with status open, lost or expiredLane, date, price you gave, contact

Your load historyShippers who moved 1–3 loads and stoppedOne-and-done accounts

Columns for your sheet: Company · Contact · Email · Phone · Lane(s) · Last contact date · What happened · Score (you'll fill Score in Step 2).

Two rules while you pull:

  1. Don't judge yet. A shipper who went quiet in March might have a new lane in October.
  2. Write down why it died, in one line, if you know: "price," "went with incumbent," "lane got cancelled," "never answered." That line decides what you say next.

When you're done, count the rows and write the number down. You'll want it later.

Step 2: Score the list so you call the right ones first

Give each shipper 0–2 points on five questions. Maximum score: 10.

What to do with the score:

  • 8–10: call this week. Personal email today, phone call in 48 hours.
  • 5–7: email sequence. Use the Step 3 cheat sheet to pick the right secret, then two or three touches over two weeks.
  • 2–4: stay in touch. One useful note a month (a market update on their lane).
  • 0–1, or anyone who asked you to stop: remove them. Respecting a "no" protects your reputation and your email deliverability.

One honest note: the score is a judgment call, not science. Its job is to make you start with the best 20 names instead of the most recent 20.

Step 3: The re-engagement playbook the biggest brokerages run

Small brokers follow up by asking for a load. The largest brokerages play a different game: they ask for a position. Here's how the inside of the business actually works, and the exact words to use.

Secret 1Don't ask for a load. Ask for a spot in the routing guide.

Shippers with steady freight tender their loads down a routing guide: a ranked list of carriers and brokers per lane. The primary gets the first offer. When the primary rejects the tender, the load falls to the next name on the list, and eventually to the spot market. The big players fight for backup positions because that's where volume leaks every time capacity gets tight.

Subject: backup on {lane}

Hi {first name}, quick one: who's your backup on {origin}–{destination} when your primary turns a load down? If that spot's open, I'd like to earn it. No volume promises needed, just first call when it's tight.

Send when: the quote went quiet because "we're covered." Covered today doesn't mean covered when the primary rejects.

Secret 2Know their bid calendar better than they do.

Most mid-size and large shippers set truckload rates through an annual RFP, and many run smaller mini-bids during the year. A broker who shows up the week after awards waits a year. The ones who win are on the invite list before the bid opens.

Subject: your next truckload bid

Hi {first name}, when's your next bid or mini-bid on truckload? I'd like to be on the invite list for {lane / region}. If you run it through a platform, point me to the right person and I'll get set up ahead of time.

Send when: any time you don't know the answer. Write the date in your CRM. Follow up 30 days before it.

Secret 3Follow up when the market turns, not when your calendar says.

Tender rejections rise when capacity tightens: end of month, end of quarter, produce season, holidays, bad weather. That's exactly when shippers are looking past their primary. Market data tools publish tender rejection trends; even without them, you'll hear it from your carriers first. That's your signal to reopen every quiet quote on that lane.

Subject: {lane} is tightening

Hi {first name}, heads up: our carriers on {lane} are getting pickier this week, {one real thing you're seeing}. If your primary starts turning loads down, I have trucks lined up out of {origin}. Want me to hold one?

Send when: you actually see it. A fake "market alert" burns trust for good.

Secret 4Every follow-up has to carry something.

The brokerages that stay in a shipper's inbox for years never write "just checking in." Every message carries a lane observation, a capacity heads-up, a rate check or a useful question. The value is the reason to reply.

Subject: {lane} this month

Hi {first name}, no ask here. What we're seeing on {lane}: {one real observation about capacity, timing or rates}. If that matches what you're dealing with, happy to price your next one as a benchmark.

Secret 5A lost quote is a service audit waiting 60 to 90 days.

When a shipper picks a cheaper option, the story isn't over. New providers get tested in the first few weeks: missed pickups, late check calls, surprise accessorials. Big brokerages log every loss with a callback date and go back to ask how it went.

Subject: how's {lane} going?

Hi {first name}, you went a different direction on {lane} back in {month}, totally fair. Honest question: how's it going? If anything's slipped, I'd like to be your second call. If it's perfect, I'll leave you alone.

Secret 6Never live or die by one contact.

One quiet contact isn't a quiet account. Enterprise brokers map two or three people per shipper: the logistics or transportation manager, the shipping lead at the plant, and procurement when bids come up. When one goes silent, another is still talking.

Subject: right person for {lane}?

Hi {first name}, I've been working with {other contact} on {company}'s {lane} freight. Are you involved in how truckload gets covered out of {plant/city}, or is there someone else I should know?

Secret 7Ask the one question that makes them talk.

Big-account reps don't open with a pitch. They open with the question the shipper actually cares about, then shut up.

Live call opener

Hi {first name}, it's {name} with {company}. We quoted your {lane} freight back in {month}. I'll be quick: when your primary can't cover that lane, what happens?

Then stop talking. Their answer tells you if there's a backup spot, a bid coming, or a service problem.

Voicemail (under 20 seconds)

Hi {first name}, {name} with {company}. Calling about backup coverage on {lane}, the one we quoted in {month}. I'll send a two-line email so you have my number: {number}.

The close-the-loop (last email in any sequence)

Subject: should I close this out?

Hi {first name}, I'll assume the timing's off and stop here. If your primary ever turns down {lane}, reply "backup" and you'll have a number from me the same day.

If a shipper tells you they already have a broker, DAT's tip is a good one: offer a free, no-obligation audit of their current rates. And ask to be their backup on one lane.

The cold call script that asks for one lane, not the whole business

The mistake most cold calls make: asking for "your freight." Nobody hands a stranger their freight. Ask for one lane, as a backup. It's a small yes.

Opener (10 seconds)

Hi {first name}, it's {name} with {company}. I'll be quick: we move {freight type} out of {their city or region}. Do you have 30 seconds, or did I catch you at a bad time?

If it's a bad time: "No problem, when's better, tomorrow morning or afternoon?" Then call back exactly then.

Discovery (let them talk)

  1. "What's moving most for you right now, and where to?"
  2. "Which lane gives you the most headaches?"
  3. "When a carrier falls off, what happens?"

The small ask

Would it make sense for me to be your backup on {the headache lane}? Next time it's tight, send it my way and I'll give you a number within the hour.

7 mistakes that kill a shipper pipeline

  1. One follow-up, then silence. Quotes rarely die from one "no." They die from no second message.
  2. "Just checking in." It gives the shipper nothing to answer. Ask about the lane instead.
  3. Asking for all their freight. Ask for one lane, as a backup.
  4. Leads living in a rep's phone. If it's not in a shared sheet or CRM, it leaves when the rep does.
  5. Leading with price. Shippers already get cheap quotes. Lead with coverage when it's tight and answering the phone.
  6. Chasing every channel at once. Two channels, every week, beats nine channels once.
  7. Ignoring a "stop." Remove them the same day. It protects your name and your email deliverability.

FAQ

What's the fastest way to find shippers as a new freight broker? Start with anyone you've already quoted or talked to, even informally, and ask about one specific lane. Then add referrals and look-alikes of your first customer. Pure cold calling works, but it's the slowest path to a first load.

How do freight brokers find direct shippers? Through referrals, industrial directories like Thomasnet, import records, Google Maps searches for businesses with dock doors, LinkedIn, and targeted cold calls. The best leads usually look like a shipper you already move.

How many times should I follow up on a freight quote? More than once. A simple rhythm: a lane question on day 1, a phone call 2 days later, a different angle at one week, and a close-the-loop email at two to four weeks. Stop right away if they ask you to.

Should I buy shipper lead lists? Only after you've worked the list you already have. Purchased lists are cold, and contact data goes stale. Your own quiet quotes are warmer and free.

How long does it take to get a first shipper? It depends on your market, niche and how many conversations you have each week. That's why the 30-day plan measures conversations, not just loads.

What should I say when a shipper says they already have a broker? "Makes sense. Who do you call when they can't cover it?" Then ask to be the backup on one lane.
