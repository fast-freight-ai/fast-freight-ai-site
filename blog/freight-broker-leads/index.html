Step 2: Answer new leads inside the hour

A new inbound lead is the most expensive thing in your inbox to ignore. Researchers writing in Harvard Business Review audited 2,241 U.S. companies and found that only 37% responded to a web lead within an hour, and the average response time was 42 hours. Firms that reached out within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as those that waited even one more hour, and more than 60 times as likely as those that waited 24 hours or longer.

That study isn't about freight, but anyone who has lost a spot load to a broker who called back first knows it applies. When a shipper sends an RFQ, they usually send it to more than one broker.

The one-hour reply (email)

Subject: re: {lane}

Hi {first name}, got it. Two quick questions so I price this right: what's the pickup window, and is this a one-time move or does it run regularly? I'll have a number back to you today.

Send when: an RFQ or form comes in and you can't price it in the next few minutes. The reply buys you time and starts the conversation. The second question tells you if this is a spot load or a lane worth fighting for.

The same-day call (if they left a number)

Hi {first name}, {name} with {company}, calling about the {lane} load you sent over. I'm working on the rate now. Quick one while I have you: who do you usually use on this lane, and what made you look around today?

That last question is the one that matters. "Our carrier fell off" and "we're shopping for price" are two very different leads.

Step 3: Qualify leads the way big brokerages do

Small brokers qualify a lead by asking "do you have freight?" Large brokerages already assume the answer is yes. What they want to know is how the shipper buys freight, because that decides whether the lead is worth a week of follow-up or a single quote.

Secret 1Find out how they buy before you price.

Shippers buy truckload in roughly three ways: contract (awarded through an annual bid and tendered down a routing guide), spot (priced load by load when the contract carrier can't cover), and project freight (one-time moves). A spot request from a shipper with a routing guide is really a question: "can you cover what my primary just rejected?" Win that, and you're a candidate for a backup position.

Hi {first name}, before I price this: is {lane} normally covered under a contract, and this one just fell through? If so, I'd like to be the name you call next time it does.

Send when: a shipper you don't know sends a single load request. It turns a one-off quote into a conversation about a position.

Secret 2Find the person behind the RFQ.

The person who sends the load request is often not the person who picks brokers. At larger shippers, a coordinator books freight, a transportation or logistics manager owns the routing guide, and procurement runs the annual bid. Enterprise reps map all three.

Hi {first name}, thanks for sending this over. When {company} sets up new brokers for {lane / region}, who makes that call? Happy to keep working through you, I just want to make sure we're set up the right way.

Send when: you've quoted a coordinator twice and still don't know who decides.

Secret 3Ask for the lane that fails, not the lane that works.

No shipper moves freight off a lane that's running smoothly. The door is the lane their current provider keeps struggling with: the tight pickup window, the rural delivery, the reefer load in produce season. That's also where brokers who answer the phone earn their reputation.

Hi {first name}, which lane gives your team the most trouble right now? Not asking for all your freight, just the one that keeps you on the phone.

Send when: a lead goes quiet after "we're covered." Covered doesn't mean happy.

Secret 4Check the credit before you chase.

Big brokerages run a credit check on a new shipper before the first load, not after the first late payment. A lead that won't pay is worse than no lead, because you still owe your carrier. Build it into your process so the question never feels personal.

Hi {first name}, great, let's get you set up. I'll send over a short credit application so we're ready to move {lane} as soon as you need us. Who in accounts payable should I copy?

Send when: the first load is agreed. It also gets you a second contact at the company.

Why purchased lead lists underperform

Bought lists aren't useless. They're just the coldest, most expensive and most fragile leads you can work. Three reasons:

1. The data goes stale fast. People change jobs, titles and email addresses all the time. ZoomInfo, which sells contact data, cites HubSpot benchmarks putting aggregate B2B database decay at 22.5% a year. A list that was accurate when it was built is less accurate every month you hold it.

2. Bounces and complaints hurt your sending domain. Emailing a stale list means bounces, and cold recipients hit "spam." Gmail enforces a spam-rate threshold on senders, and bulk senders (more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail addresses) must offer one-click unsubscribe and process it within two days (Google). Burn your domain on a cheap list and your warm follow-ups start landing in spam too.

3. The rules still apply to B2B. The FTC is direct about it: CAN-SPAM "makes no exception for business-to-business email." Every commercial email needs your valid physical postal address and a way to opt out, and you must honor an opt-out within 10 business days (FTC). This isn't legal advice, just the baseline to know before you send.

8 mistakes that waste good leads

  1. Buying before mining. A cold list before your own quiet quotes is paying for the leads you already have.
  2. Answering tomorrow. A new RFQ that waits overnight is often already covered.
  3. Pricing without asking how they buy. A spot quote with no question attached is a one-and-done by design.
  4. One contact per account. When your one contact goes quiet, the account does too.
  5. "Just checking in." Every follow-up should carry a lane question, a capacity note or a rate check.
  6. Skipping the credit check. A lead that doesn't pay costs you more than no lead.
  7. Blasting an unverified list. Bounces and spam complaints follow you to your warm emails.
  8. Not tracking the source. If you don't know where your last five shippers came from, you can't get five more.

FAQ

How do freight brokers get leads? From four places: shippers they've already quoted, new inbound requests, look-alikes of their best customers found through free sources, and purchased lists. The first two convert fastest because the shipper already knows your name.

Where can I find free freight broker leads? Your own quote log and load history, consignees on your loads, referrals, Google Alerts, USDA business listings, MacRAE's Blue Book, import records like ImportYeti, conference exhibitor lists and LinkedIn.

Are freight broker lead lists worth buying? Sometimes, but only after you've worked your warm leads. Contact data decays over time, and emailing stale addresses can hurt your sender reputation. Ask when each record was last verified, and test a small batch first.

How fast should I respond to a new freight lead? Within the hour if you can. Research in Harvard Business Review found firms that responded within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify a lead as those that waited even an hour longer.

What's the difference between a warm and a cold freight lead? A warm lead has had contact with you: a quote, a call, a booked load. A cold lead has never heard of you. Warm leads need a reason to reply; cold leads need a reason to trust you first.

Does CAN-SPAM apply to cold emails to shippers? Yes. The FTC says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. Include your physical postal address, give a clear way to opt out, and honor opt-outs within 10 business days.
